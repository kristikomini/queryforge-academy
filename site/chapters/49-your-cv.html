Your CV, LinkedIn and this project

Italian CV conventions, the GDPR consent line, and how to write a bullet that contains a fact.

No advert asked for this

The advert never asks for a good CV. The filter that reads it does.

Who reads it, and for how long

🧒 Simple

The first person to look at your CV is not the person you would work for. They have forty of them, they are looking for reasons to make the pile smaller, and they will spend less than a minute on yours. Everything below is about surviving that minute.

🎓 Three readers, three different jobs
  1. An ATS or a keyword search. Which is why the exact words from the advert matter, and why a two-column PDF with the skills in a sidebar sometimes parses to nothing.
  2. A recruiter, matching against a checklist. They are not evaluating your SQL; they are checking whether the words are present and whether you are plausibly available.
  3. The technical manager, who reads it for two minutes before the interview and takes their questions from it. Everything on your CV is an invitation. Do not write anything you would not enjoy being asked about.

The consent line, verbatim

“Autorizzo il trattamento dei miei dati personali ai sensi del Regolamento UE 2016/679 (GDPR) e del D.Lgs. 196/2003 come modificato dal D.Lgs. 101/2018.”
Small print, at the bottom. Strictly it is not legally required for a CV you sent voluntarily — but its absence is read as unfamiliarity with local convention, which is exactly the impression you do not want to give in the first thirty seconds. It costs one line.

The bullet that contains a fact

🎓 Presence, action, result

Almost every weak CV bullet describes presence — that you were there and the technology existed nearby. A strong one describes a result, with a number.

✔ Where a junior finds numbers

You do not need commercial experience to have numbers. Row counts in a project database. Query time before and after. Number of tables in a schema you designed. Questions in a course you completed. Any specific quantity beats every adjective — and the honest framing (“personal project”) costs you nothing, because the reader's question is whether you can talk about it, not whether you were paid.

A structure that works

⚠ Never use a self-rating bar

The five-star or 80%-filled-circle skill graphic is the single most disliked convention among technical readers. It communicates nothing — 80% of what? — and it invites the interviewer to disprove it. Write the level in words with a concrete claim attached instead.

The project section is the whole point

🎓 Why a junior CV should lead with a project

“No commercial experience” is answered by something you built that can be questioned. Not a tutorial you followed — a thing with decisions in it that you can defend. The reference database shipped with this course exists precisely for this: a schema with a state machine, optimistic concurrency, a transactional outbox, deterministic seed data and tests against a real engine. Every one of those is a decision an interviewer can ask about, and having an answer is what commercial experience is a proxy for.

✔ Make it three minutes from CV to running

A README with a one-command setup, a diagram, and three sentences on why each non-obvious decision was made. Interviewers do look — and the ones who do are the ones you want to work for. A repository they cannot run in three minutes is a repository they will not run.

LinkedIn, briefly

  • Headline: the role, not the aspiration. “Database Developer · SQL Server · T-SQL” — not “Passionate about data”.
  • Location set to the target area. Recruiters filter by it, hard. If you are willing to move to Bologna, say Bologna.
  • “Open to work” — the recruiters-only setting is worth switching on; it is genuinely how a lot of first contact happens in Italy.
  • Skills section matching the advert vocabulary, because search runs on it.
  • An Italian profile, or bilingual. The recruiters searching for you are searching in Italian.

The cover letter

Short, specific, and about them

Three paragraphs. Why this company — one specific, checkable sentence proving you read something. Why you — the one or two requirements you match best, with the evidence. What next — availability and a clear closing line. Under 200 words. A generic letter is worse than none, because it demonstrates the thing it claims to disprove.

Interview questions this chapter answers

Tell me about this project on your CV.

The question every project line invites, which is why every project line must be defensible. Two sentences on what it is, then go straight to a decision and its trade-off — “orders use optimistic concurrency with a version column rather than locking, because the editing is done by people and I did not want a lock held across someone's thinking time”.

You have no commercial experience. Why should we take you?

Point at something concrete and invite them into it: a schema with a state machine, optimistic concurrency and a transactional outbox, tested against a real engine in CI. The claim is not that it equals commercial experience — it is that every decision in it can be questioned, and being able to answer is what the experience requirement is a proxy for.

How would you rate your SQL, one to ten?

Refuse the number politely and replace it with scope: “I am comfortable with joins, window functions, CTEs and reading an execution plan, and I have tuned queries by making an index cover them. What I have not done is administer a production instance — backups and high availability I know in theory only.” A boundary is more credible than a score.
