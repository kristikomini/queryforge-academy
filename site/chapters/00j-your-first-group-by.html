Counting things: your first GROUP BY

From “list the orders” to “how many orders per customer” — the step where SQL stops being a filter.

Every reporting question anybody will ever ask you is a GROUP BY. Chapter 10 covers the traps — HAVING, conditional aggregation, ROLLUP. This one covers the idea, and it is the last chapter of Part 0: after it, the course proper begins.

Many rows in, one row out

🧒 Simple

Put the cards into piles — one pile per customer. Now, instead of handing back the cards, hand back one slip of paper per pile: the customer's name, and how many cards were in their pile. The piling up is GROUP BY. The counting is COUNT. You get one row per pile, not one row per card.

🎓 Professional

GROUP BY partitions the rows surviving WHERE into groups sharing the listed values, and collapses each group into a single output row. After it has run, only two things are legal in SELECT: the grouping columns, and aggregates over the rest. Every other column no longer has a single value to show, which is why asking for one is an error rather than a guess.

  1. GROUP BY collapses rows sharing a value into one output row each; the result's row count is no longer the table's.
  2. After grouping, only the grouping columns and aggregates are legal in SELECT — anything else has many values and no rule for choosing.
  3. COUNT(*) counts rows and is never NULL; COUNT(col) counts non-NULL values of that column.
  4. SUM, AVG, MIN and MAX ignore NULLs, and every aggregate but COUNT returns NULL over an empty set.
  5. WHERE filters rows before grouping; HAVING filters groups after it.
  6. A group with no rows does not exist — customers with no orders are simply absent, which needs a LEFT JOIN to fix.

What is the difference between COUNT(*) and COUNT(column)?

COUNT(*) counts rows and never looks at a value, so it is never NULL and is never affected by them. COUNT(column) counts rows where that column is not NULL. Across a LEFT JOIN the difference is decisive: COUNT(*) counts the null-padded row as 1, and COUNT(o.id) correctly reports 0.

Why can you not put an aggregate in the WHERE clause?

Because WHERE runs before GROUP BY: at that point there are no groups, so there is nothing for COUNT(*) to count. Filtering on an aggregate is what HAVING is for, and it runs immediately after the grouping.
