Diagnosing a slow database in production

A method, not a guess: wait statistics, the top-ten list, blocking chains, and what to look at first.

From the advert

“Esperienza su ambienti di produzione” — this chapter is what “production experience” actually consists of: a method for the twenty minutes after somebody says “the system is slow”.

Ask three questions before touching anything

🧒 Simple

Somebody says “the car is making a noise”. Before you open the bonnet: is it always, or only when turning? Did it start today? Does it do it when the radio is off? Three questions, and you have halved the search.

🎓 The three questions
  1. Everything, or one thing? Everything slow points at a resource — CPU, memory, disk, network — or at blocking. One thing slow points at that query, its plan or its indexes.
  2. Since when, and what changed? A deploy, a data load, a growth threshold crossed, a statistics update, an index rebuild. Almost everything has a “since”.
  3. Slow, or waiting? A query burning CPU and a query blocked on a lock look identical from the application and have nothing in common. Wait statistics answer this in one query, and it is the highest-value question of the three.

✔ ASYNC_NETWORK_IO is the one worth memorising

It almost never means the network is slow. It means the database produced rows faster than the application consumed them — usually because the client is fetching a million rows and processing them one at a time in a loop, or paging through a grid it never finishes. The database is being blamed for the application's behaviour, and being able to demonstrate that calmly, with a number, is a genuinely valuable thing to be able to do.

The top-ten list

Run it twice, a few minutes apart

These counters are cumulative since the server started, so the top of the list may be a batch job that ran at 03:00 and is irrelevant now. Snapshot, wait five minutes, snapshot again and diff. What grew is what is happening now, and that is a different list.

The blocking chain

⚠ The head of the chain is often idle in transaction

A session that ran one UPDATE, never committed, and is now sitting idle — because the application opened a transaction, called an external service, and is waiting for a timeout. It is using no CPU and running no query, which is why it does not appear in any "expensive queries" list, and it is holding the lock everyone else is queued behind. Look for long-running transactions, not just long-running queries.

The twenty-minute checklist

✔ In this order
  1. Is it the database at all? Application logs, the network, the disk. Do not assume it is yours because somebody said "the database is slow".
  2. Blocking chain. One query, thirty seconds. Rules out or confirms the most common cause of a sudden total freeze.
  3. Wait statistics. Slow or waiting, and waiting for what.
  4. Top queries by total time, diffed over five minutes. What is actually running now.
  5. Server resources. CPU, memory, disk latency, and how much free space the log volume has.
  6. What changed. Deploys, loads, index or statistics jobs, data growth.
  7. Only now, look at an individual query's plan (chapter 27).

The thing to do before the incident

“Is 400 ms bad?” has no answer without a baseline. Record, weekly: the top ten queries by total duration, wait statistics, index usage, table sizes, and the slowest report's runtime. Ten minutes a week, and the day something goes wrong you are comparing against last Tuesday instead of guessing. Proposing this in an interview says you have been on the wrong side of not having it.

  1. Everything slow means a resource or blocking; one thing slow means that query.
  2. “Slow or waiting?” is the highest-value question, and wait statistics answer it in one query.
  3. ASYNC_NETWORK_IO means the client is not consuming rows fast enough — it is the application's problem.
  4. CXPACKET is usually the symptom of a big scan, not a cause to tune away.
  5. Sort the top-query list by total time and diff two snapshots. Cumulative counters describe the whole uptime.
  6. Find the head of the blocking chain: blocked by nobody, blocking everyone.
  7. The head is often idle-in-transaction, so it appears in no expensive-query list.
  8. Look at the individual plan last, not first.
  9. Without a baseline, every number is unfalsifiable. Record one weekly.

Interview questions this chapter answers

The application is slow. Walk me through what you do.

First establish whether it is everything or one thing, and what changed and when. Then a blocking-chain query, which takes thirty seconds and catches the most common cause of a sudden freeze. Then wait statistics to find out whether we are slow or waiting, and for what. Then the top queries by total duration, diffed over a few minutes so I see what is happening now. Server resources. And only then an individual execution plan.

What are wait statistics and why start there?

They record what the engine spent time waiting for, aggregated by cause. They separate "we are doing too much work" from "we are queued behind something" — which are indistinguishable from the application and have entirely different fixes. It is one query and it usually points straight at the category of problem.

Everything is blocked and the head of the chain is running nothing. What is going on?

A session that is idle inside an open transaction — it made a change, never committed, and is probably waiting on an external call or a user. It holds its locks and uses no resources, so it appears in no expensive-query list. I would look for the oldest open transaction, not the longest-running query, and then fix the application pattern that leaves one open.
