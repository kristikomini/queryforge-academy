OLTP vs OLAP and dimensional modelling

Star schemas, fact and dimension tables, grain, and slowly changing dimensions type 1 and type 2.

From the advert

“Reporting con SSRS e Power BI” — the query behind a report is usually the easy part. Designing the model the reports sit on is the job.

Two workloads, two designs

🧒 Simple

A shop till and a shop's annual accounts want opposite things. The till needs to add one sale, right now, without disturbing anybody. The accounts need to add up two million sales, and do not mind waiting a minute. Designing one thing to do both means it does neither well.

OLTPOLAP
Unit of workOne orderTwo years of orders
ReadsA few rows by keyMillions, aggregated
WritesConstant, small, concurrentBulk, scheduled, single-writer
DesignNormalised — 3NFDenormalised — star schema
IndexesNarrow, selective, manyFew; columnstore, or none at all
Optimised forLatency and concurrencyThroughput per scan
🎓 Why analytical queries want a different shape

Normalisation exists to prevent update anomalies from concurrent piecemeal writes. A warehouse is loaded in bulk by one controlled process, so those anomalies largely cannot arise — and the cost of normalisation, an eleven-table join on every question, is paid on every query instead. A star schema trades storage and update flexibility for fewer joins and sequential scans, which is the right trade for that workload and the wrong one for a till.

The star schema

🎓 Grain is the first decision and the one that matters

What does one row of the fact table represent? One order line? One order? One day per product? Everything else follows: which dimensions apply, which measures are additive, what the row count will be. Getting it wrong is not a tuning problem, it is a rebuild — and "what is the grain?" is the question a data-warehouse interviewer is waiting to hear you ask.

TermMeans
FactA measurement: quantity, revenue, duration. Numeric and usually additive
DimensionThe context you slice by: who, what, when, where
GrainWhat one fact row means. Decide it first, write it down
AdditiveSummable across every dimension — revenue
Semi-additiveSummable across some — a stock level sums over products but not over time
Non-additiveA ratio or percentage. Store the numerator and denominator; compute the ratio on read
SnowflakeNormalised dimensions. More joins, less redundancy, usually not worth it
Conformed dimensionOne dim_customer shared by several fact tables, so figures reconcile across reports
⚠ Trap — averaging a semi-additive measure

Summing a daily stock level over a month gives a number with no meaning. Summing a percentage across regions gives a wrong one. Non-additive measures must be stored as their components: keep delivered_count and total_count, and compute the rate in the report. Storing the percentage is the commonest fatal mistake in a warehouse design, because every subsequent aggregation of it is silently wrong.

Slowly changing dimensions

🧒 Simple

A customer moves from Modena to Milano. Do last year's sales now count as Milano sales, or do they stay Modena sales because that is where they happened? Both answers are defensible; the disaster is not deciding, because then different reports give different totals.

TypeBehaviourUse when
Type 1Overwrite. History is lostCorrecting a mistake — a misspelled name
Type 2New row, with valid_from/valid_to and an is_current flagThe change is real and history matters. The default
Type 3A previous_city column beside the current oneYou need exactly one prior value. Rare
 
-- Type 2 in practice. The SURROGATE key is what makes it work:
dim_customer
 customer_key  customer_id  name        city     valid_from   valid_to     is_current
 ------------  -----------  ----------  -------  -----------  -----------  ----------
 501           42           Rossi SpA   Modena   2024-01-01   2026-06-30   0
 902           42           Rossi SpA   Milano   2026-07-01   9999-12-31   1

-- A fact row records customer_key 501, so it is permanently attached to the
-- version of the customer that existed when the sale happened. This is the whole
-- reason a warehouse uses surrogate keys instead of the source system's ids —
-- a natural key cannot express "this row, as it was then".
✔ Say this in an interview

“Dimension surrogate keys are not just for narrowness — they are what lets a fact reference a version of a dimension row, which is how type 2 history works at all.” That one sentence covers surrogate keys, SCDs and grain in a way that is hard to fake.

Where this lands in the Italian market

Most jobs advertising “Power BI” or “SSRS” are asking you to be the layer underneath: the views, the summary tables, the nightly load and the dim_date. A useful, concrete thing to know is that Power BI's own model is a star schema — its engine (VertiPaq) is columnar and optimised for exactly this shape, and a report built on one wide flat table or on a normalised OLTP schema will be slow in ways no amount of DAX fixes. “I would give Power BI a star schema, not a view over the OLTP tables” is a strong, specific answer. 

-- The reference database already has a dim_date. Build the smallest possible
-- fact table from it and see the shape:
SELECT d.year, d.month, r.name AS region, COUNT(*) AS orders,
       SUM(o.total_cents)/100.0 AS revenue_eur
FROM orders o
JOIN dim_date d ON d.d = date(o.placed_at)
JOIN customers c ON c.id = o.customer_id
JOIN cities   ci ON ci.id = c.city_id
JOIN regions   r ON r.id = ci.region_id
WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
GROUP BY d.year, d.month, r.name
ORDER BY d.year, d.month, revenue_eur DESC;
  1. OLTP optimises latency and concurrency; OLAP optimises throughput per scan. One design cannot do both.
  2. A warehouse is denormalised because it is bulk-loaded by one process, so update anomalies largely cannot arise.
  3. Decide the grain first and write it down. Everything else follows from it.
  4. Facts are measurements; dimensions are the context you slice by.
  5. Never store a ratio as a measure. Store the numerator and denominator.
  6. Semi-additive measures — stock levels, balances — cannot be summed over time.
  7. Type 1 overwrites, type 2 adds a versioned row. Type 2 is the default when history matters.
  8. Surrogate dimension keys are what let a fact point at a version of a dimension row.
  9. A conformed dimension is what makes two reports agree.
  10. Power BI's engine wants a star schema. A view over the OLTP schema will be slow whatever the DAX.

Interview questions this chapter answers

What is a star schema and why is it denormalised?

A central fact table of measurements surrounded by dimension tables you slice by, so every query is one join per dimension. It is denormalised because the workload is bulk-loaded and read with wide aggregate scans — the anomalies normalisation prevents cannot really arise, and the joins it would cost are paid on every query.

A customer changes city. How do you handle it in the warehouse?

First ask whether historical sales should follow them. If not — usually the case — it is a type 2 slowly changing dimension: close the current row with a valid_to, insert a new one with a new surrogate key, and leave existing facts pointing at the old key. If it is a correction rather than a change, type 1 and overwrite.

Why not store a conversion rate in the fact table?

Because it is non-additive: summing rates is meaningless and averaging averages is wrong. I store the numerator and denominator as separate additive measures and compute the rate at report time, so it is correct at every level of aggregation.
